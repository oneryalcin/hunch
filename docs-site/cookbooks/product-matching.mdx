---
title: "Match two product catalogues"
description: "One shop calls it a PSLX350H, another a PS-LX350H. Here is how to teach a program that those are the same turntable, and that two cameras one digit apart are not."
---

Here's a puzzle. One shop sells a `Sony Turntable - PSLX350H`. Another sells a `Sony PS-LX350H Belt-Drive Turntable`. You and I can see at a glance that it's the same record player.

Now these two: a `Sony Cyber-Shot Black Digital Camera - DSCT500B` and a `Sony Cyber-shot DSC-T300 Digital Camera - Black`. They look even more alike. But one is a T500 and the other a T300. Different cameras.

Suppose you have two catalogues like this, a thousand products each, and you need to know which products are the same. How would you write that program?

## The first idea: compare the codes

Almost everyone starts the same way, and it's a good start. Products have model codes. Strip out the hyphens, and `PSLX350H` matches `PS-LX350H`. When the codes don't help, count how many words the two names share.

This works surprisingly often. It also fails in a way that's worth looking at closely:

```text
Garmin 010-10723-02 Carrying Case      the rules pick   Garmin USB Cable - 010-10723-01
                                       the real match   Garmin Leather GPS Case
```

The codes are one digit apart, so the rules say the carrying case is a USB cable. No person would make that mistake. A case is a case, and a cable is a cable. But "is this a case?" isn't a question about characters. It's a question about meaning, and that's the one thing rules can't ask.

So the rules are useful, but not as the judge. They're a sieve. Comparing every product with every product is over a million pairs, and nearly all of them are nonsense: a turntable against photo paper. The rules throw those out cheaply and keep, for each product, a handful of plausible partners. Then something that understands meaning looks at what's left.

## Asking the question

That something is a question, asked once for every pair: are these the same product?

You could write a loop that sends each pair to a language model. Plenty of people do. It works on the first day, and then the questions start. You change the prompt: did it get better, or did it just change? You run the loop again tomorrow: do you pay for every pair again? The model says "yes" to a pair: how sure is it, and should anyone look?

hunch is built around those questions. The question lives in a file, next to your code:

```yaml same_product.yml
judgment: same_product
model: jev-1.13.0
source: py(candidates.py:pairs)       # the pairs the rules kept
key: id
state: [abt_name, abt_description, abt_price, buy_name, buy_manufacturer, buy_description, buy_price]
questions:
  same:
    type: noul                         # a yes-or-no question
    instructions: >-
      Two shops list a product: `abt_name` (with `abt_description` and `abt_price`) and `buy_name` (with
      `buy_manufacturer`, `buy_description` and `buy_price`). Are they the same product: the same maker and the same
      model? A different model number, size or capacity is a different product, and so is an accessory made for it.
      The two shops word names differently, and a price may be missing. A shop may also add its own letters to the
      end of the same model's code (AB123 in one shop, AB123XY in the other): when the rest of the code and the
      product agree, it is the same product. But when both listings name a colour and the colours differ, they are
      different products.
    gold: gold_same                    # the known answer, for testing
    act: 0.9
```

Every answer comes back with a number: how sure. Here is the turntable against three of the candidates the rules kept for it:

| Candidate | Same product? | How sure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sony PS-LX350H Belt-Drive Turntable | yes | 0.95 |
| Sony PSLX300USB USB Record Turntable | no | 0.98 |
| Sony Print Paper - SVMF40P | no | 0.99 |

Look at the middle row. Same maker, nearly the same code, the same kind of thing, and it's still a different turntable. That's exactly where the rules get lost, and the question doesn't.

Every answer is also kept, filed under exactly what was asked. Run it again tomorrow and nothing is asked twice.

## Being sure is the point

That "how sure" number changes what you can do with the answers.

Without it, you have two bad choices: trust every answer, or check every answer. With it, you can say something more sensible: act on the answers that are sure, and show me the rest. That's what `act: 0.9` in the spec means. Think of a careful colleague who signs off on what they're certain of, and leaves the doubtful ones on your desk.

Here, about nine in ten pairs are signed off. The rest wait for a person.

## Back to SQL

A "yes" for each pair isn't quite a list of matches yet. A product can have two candidates that both look right, and you want one. That last step is ordinary data work, so it belongs where the data is.

hunch turns the spec into a function DuckDB can call, so the question's answer is just another column. Then a query keeps, for each product, its likeliest partner:

```sql
select * from (select * from decided where label = 'yes'
               qualify row_number() over (partition by abt_id order by p_yes desc, rank::int, buy_id) = 1)
qualify row_number() over (partition by buy_id order by p_yes desc, rank::int, abt_id) = 1;
```

Step back and look at what we have. A rule in SQL, a question in a file, a query in SQL. The judgment in the middle, the part that used to be a person squinting at two product names, is now a piece of your pipeline like any other.

And it pays off where it matters. On the benchmark these catalogues come from, the rules alone make 126 wrong matches. With the question in the middle, 9.

## The fix that looked right

This is the part I'd most like you to take away.

The first version of the question said nothing about shops adding their own letters to a code. So when one shop listed a wall mount as `PM1327BK` and the other as `PM1327`, the model said: different products. Fair enough, given what it was told.

The fix seemed obvious. Add a sentence: shops add letters "for a colour, a finish or a region", and then it's the same product. You'd approve that in a code review. I would have.

Before shipping it, `hunch diff` asked the new question of every pair and compared the answers with the old ones:

```text
same: 68/5396 rows flip (40 within noise band)
  gold accuracy on the 5286 shared rows with gold 98.6% → 98.3%  (✓ 23 fixed, ✗ 40 broken, 0 wrong both times, 5 without gold)
```

It fixed 23 pairs and broke 40. The broken ones were cameras. A silver Cyber-shot and a gold Cyber-shot are two different products, and "a colour" in the new sentence had just told the model they were one.

Nobody reading that sentence would have seen it coming. The only way to see it was to try the edit on everything, before it shipped. That's what `diff` is for, and it's the same instinct as running your tests before you merge.

The sentence that shipped keeps the letters and draws the line at colours: if both listings name a colour and they differ, they're different products. `diff` again: 35 fixed, 4 broken.

That's the real lesson here, bigger than product matching. The words in a question are code. A small edit can break things far from where you made it. So you treat them like code: kept in a file, tested, and checked on every row before they change.

## Try it

```sh
uv run --extra sql python prototype/examples/product_matching/match.py    # about $0.14 the first time, then free
```

To match your own lists, point the `read_csv` lines in `candidates.py` at your files and keep the question. Then [review](/guides/review) a few dozen of your own pairs, and `hunch test` will tell you how often it's right on your products, not ours.

## How it was measured

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="The data">
    [Abt-Buy](https://dbs.uni-leipzig.de/research/projects/benchmark-datasets-for-entity-resolution), a benchmark from the University of Leipzig (Creative Commons): 1,081 products from Abt, 1,092 from Buy, 1,097 known matches. Comparing all of them is 1.18 million pairs; `candidates.py` keeps five per Abt product, 5,396 pairs, which hold 1,061 of the 1,097 matches. The 36 lost share a few words with their match, but five other listings outrank them. Asking all 5,396 took about 90 seconds and $0.14.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The results, in full">
    Against the benchmark's answer key: each product's best candidate by the rules alone, 955 of 1,081 right; the rules, the question and the query, 971 of 980 right (precision 99.1%, recall 88.5%); keeping only matches above the bar, 682 of 682. 4,797 of the 5,396 pairs clear `act: 0.9`. For 51 products the rules' two best candidates tie and the lower id wins. Since 16 products have more than one true match, one match per product can reach at most 1,044 of the 1,097.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Checking the answer key">
    The first version of the question disagreed with the benchmark's answer key on 180 pairs, and its most confident disagreements looked like the key's mistakes (a 500GB drive matched to a 1TB one). A blind panel of three AI reviewers (one Claude Opus, two Claude Sonnet, one reading in reverse order) judged those 180 and a random 100, seeing what the model saw and neither the model's answer nor the key's. On the 180, the key was right 69 times and the model 22; 81 could not be decided from the listings, and on 8 the reviewers split. On the random 100, the panel agreed with both on 96. Two later rounds covered the new wordings' fresh disagreements (11 pairs, then 4). The reviewers' prompt is in `prototype/review_panel/product_matching/PROMPT.md`; it told them a trailing colour or region suffix can be formatting, a hint the first version of the question lacked.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Accuracy, with its interval">
    With the key corrected by the panel, `hunch test` estimates the question is right on 99.2% of pairs (95% CI 95.4–99.2%), from 100 random reviews and every disagreement. Of the 4,797 pairs above the bar, 2 are wrong against that gold, both a confident "no" for the same product under a shop's own code (a Maytag microwave and a Sanus wall mount). Against the uncorrected key 15 are, and the panel judged 12 of those the key's mistake.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The question was tuned on the pairs it is scored on">
    The code-suffix pattern was found in the panel's verdicts, and each version was judged by `diff` against the same gold, so the final numbers are somewhat optimistic for listings like these that it hasn't seen. The first two versions are kept as `first/same_product.yml` and `tried/same_product.yml`, so both diffs can be run again for free.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What did not work">
    A small local model trained with [`hunch distill`](/guides/distill), which would make matching free, separated matches from non-matches barely better than a coin (AUROC 0.576, where the question scores 1.000). It reads both listings as one piece of text and loses whether `EX85` and `EX81` are the same code. And the panel is not people: on the hard pairs, one reviewer said "same" on 60 pairs the other two called undecidable. For your own catalogues, a person reviewing the pairs below the bar is the better answer key.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
