A search assistant is asked how automotive technicians get paid. It finds three passages and writes, among other things:

> The specific amount of pay varies by location, with the highest average pay in Alaska (\$23.70 per hour or \$49,400 per year) and the lowest average pay in Mississippi (\$18.60 per hour or \$38,900 per year).

Alaska is in the passages, with exactly those figures. Mississippi is not in them at all. The model made up a state and two numbers, in the same calm voice as the true ones.

How would your code tell?

<Info>
This happens wherever a model answers from text it retrieved: search, support bots, assistants over internal documents. Retrieval and writing are yours. hunch is for the last step, deciding whether the answer stays inside its sources.
</Info>

## Try it first

Read the passages, then the answer. Is everything it says somewhere in the passages? Then see what hunch said, and what the people who annotated the answer marked.

<AnswerCheck puzzles={RAG_PUZZLES} />

Some are easy once you look: a line number or a peppermint oil tip appears from nowhere. Others are a matter of taste. Is turning 79°F into 26°C adding a fact? That question comes back at the end.

## 1. One row per answer

A row holds what the assistant saw and what it said. The example uses 900 answers from RAGTruth, a public dataset in which people marked every part of an answer the passages don't support. Here is the Mississippi answer as a row:

| Column | For the Mississippi answer |
| --- | --- |
| `question` | how do automotive technicians get paid |
| `passages` | three passages, starting “passage 1:In short, whether mechanics and technicians are entitled to overtime…” |
| `answer` | “Based on the provided passages, automotive technicians can get paid in different ways…” |
| `gold_unsupported` | `yes`: the annotators marked something in it |

`gold_unsupported` is the known answer. The check never reads it; it is what the check is tested against.

## 2. Write the question

One file says what to ask about each answer, and what the model may read:

```yaml answer_support.yml
# simplified: the file also has a test and two pinned examples
judgment: answer_support
model: jev-1.13.0
source: ragtruth_qa.csv
key: id
state: [question, passages, answer]
clip: {passages: 12000}   # characters, at most
questions:
  unsupported:
    type: noul            # yes or no, with how sure
    instructions: >-
      `answer` was written to answer `question` using
      only `passages`. Does `answer` state anything that
      `passages` do not support: a fact, number, name,
      date or detail that is missing from the passages,
      or that contradicts them? Saying the passages don't
      contain the answer is supported. General wording
      and restating the question are supported.
    gold: gold_unsupported
    act: {yes: 1.0, no: 0.7}
```

`state` is all the model sees: the question, the passages and the answer, never the annotators' mark. `act` is the rule for what happens next, and step 4 is about it.

## 3. Run it

```sh
hunch run prototype/examples/rag_answers/answer_support.yml \
  --max-cost 0.05
```

All 900 answers took 15 seconds and about three cents. Every answer is kept, so running it again is free. For the Mississippi answer the check says `yes`, something is unsupported, and it is 0.93 sure.

## 4. Pass only what it's sure is supported

`act` has two sides. On the `no` side, 0.7 means an answer the check is at least 0.7 sure is supported goes straight to the user. On the `yes` side, 1.0 is a bar no answer reaches, so the check never blocks an answer by itself. Everything it isn't sure about goes to a person.

On these 900, a little over half go straight through, and 4 of them hold something the annotators marked. The rest wait for a person, and they include 156 of the 160 marked answers.

## Stricter than the annotators

Look again at the bars: the teal one is longer for every model. When the check says an answer is unsupported, the annotators agree only about half the time.

Its most confident disagreement, at 0.96, is an answer about how the body's systems work together. It summarises the three passages faithfully, then adds a list of its own: "The skeletal system provides support and structure for the body", the digestive system breaks food down into nutrients, the nervous system coordinates movement and thought. All of it is textbook biology. None of it is in the passages.

The annotators let textbook knowledge pass. The question, which asks about the passages only, does not. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions, and whether your assistant may add what everyone knows is a product decision.

That is why the dial is lopsided. When the check is sure an answer is supported, it is almost always right. When it says unsupported, it is often only being strict. So pass what it's sure of and show the rest to a person. Their verdicts, saved by [`hunch review`](/guides/review), become the known answers, and the next `hunch test` measures the check against your rule instead of RAGTruth's.

## Use it on your data

```sh
hunch init rag-answers
```

This copies the spec, the 900 answers, two pinned examples and the measured results into `rag_answers/`. Replace the rows with your own questions, retrieved passages and answers, [review](/guides/review) a few dozen, and `hunch test` tells you how the check does on your data. In an app, ask before an answer is shown:

```python
a = hunch.judge("answer_support.yml", question=q,
                passages=passages, answer=answer)["unsupported"]
if a["label"] == "no" and a["route"] == "act":  # sure it's supported
    show(answer)
else:
    hold_for_review(answer, a["p"])
```
