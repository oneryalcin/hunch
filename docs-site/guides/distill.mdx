![A hand copies check marks from a large ledger into a small notebook. A note with a question mark lies beside the ledger.](/images/distill-copying.webp)

A new colleague on a support desk doesn't answer every ticket on day one. They watch a few thousand get sorted, then take the easy ones and pass the odd ones back.

`hunch distill` trains a small model the same way. The experienced colleague is your engine, the model that answers your questions today. On this page that is Jev. The sorted tickets are already in your store, because hunch keeps every answer. The small model is called the student. It runs on your machine in about 3 milliseconds and costs nothing per answer.

The example on this page sorts a bank customer's message into one of 77 intents, such as `card_arrival` or `change_pin`. On 385 messages it had never seen, the student kept 297 for itself, three in four, and got 4 of them wrong. Jev answered the other 88. Graded against a reviewed answer key, the two together were right on 97.7% of the messages. Jev alone was right on 97.9%.

How does a model that small get that close?

## Your store already holds the lessons

Every time hunch asks your engine a question, it keeps the answer in the [store](/concepts#every-answer-is-stored-under-its-exact-input), next to the exact row it was asked about.

After a few thousand rows, the store holds a pile of worked examples. Each one says: here is a message, and here is how Jev answered it. A worked example is what a learner needs.

Training a small model to copy a large model's answers is called distillation. The large model is the teacher. The small one is the student.

Some rows carry a better answer than the teacher's. hunch calls an answer you trust [gold](/concepts#gold-is-the-answer-you-trust). Gold comes from an answer key in your data, or from your own verdict in `hunch review`. Where a row has gold, the student learns the gold answer. Everywhere else it learns the teacher's.

![Every answer the engine gave is in your store. hunch distill turns stored rows and their answers into worked examples and trains the student. One in five rows that have gold is held back, never shown to the student, and used by hunch test to grade it.](/images/distill-lessons.svg)

One in five of the rows that have gold is held back and never shown to the student. A student has to be graded on rows it has not learned from, and these are kept for that.

Distilling only reads the store. It asks the engine nothing, so it costs nothing.

## A student reads, then decides

A student has two parts.

The first part reads. It is a ready-made model called an encoder, and it turns a message into a list of 1,024 numbers. hunch downloads the encoder once and never changes it. That is what people mean when they call an encoder frozen.

The second part decides. For each question in your spec, hunch trains one small layer. The layer weighs the 1,024 numbers and gives a probability to each possible answer.

![The message "My card hasn't arrived yet" goes into the encoder, which is ready-made and never changed. It comes out as 1,024 numbers. One small layer, trained by hunch distill, turns them into a probability for each of the 77 answers: card_arrival 0.94, card_delivery_estimate 0.05, and 0.00 for the other 75 together.](/images/distill-student.svg)

The layer is the only part `distill` trains. That is why a finished student is two files, 391 KB together, small enough to commit beside the spec. The encoder is a separate 92 MB download, shared by every student that reads with it.

## Similar messages land close together

Why is one small layer enough to sort bank messages into 77 intents? Because the encoder has already done most of the work.

An encoder gives similar lists of numbers to messages that mean similar things. "Where is my card?" and "My card hasn't arrived yet" score 0.87 for closeness, where 1 means identical. "Where is my card?" and "How do I change my PIN?" score 0.66.

You can see this. Below are 206 of the messages the student learned from, each drawn as one mark. Its position comes from its 1,024 numbers, flattened to two so that they fit on a page. Its shape and colour show the intent Jev gave it.

Messages about exchange rates gather in one place, and failed top-ups in another. Nothing in the encoder is about your question. It places messages by what they mean, and the groups follow.

The two card intents overlap. One is about a card that has not arrived, and the other is about how long delivery takes. Customers ask both in much the same words.

Now the layer's job is easy to describe. It learns where one group ends and the next begins.

The picture understates how well that works, because two numbers can hold only part of what 1,024 do. On this flat map, 87.9% of the messages sit nearest the centre of their own group. With all 1,024 numbers, 96.1% do.

## A student knows when it is unsure

The layer doesn't pick one answer. It gives every possible answer a probability, and the probabilities add up to 1. The student's answer is the most probable one, and that probability is its confidence.

For "My card hasn't arrived yet", the student gives `card_arrival` 0.94 and `card_delivery_estimate` 0.05. It is sure.

For "When will my card get here?", it gives `card_delivery_estimate` 0.74 and `card_arrival` 0.25. The message could belong to either of the two overlapping groups, and the student's numbers say so.

A confidence is useful only if it is honest. When a student says 0.9 on a hundred answers, about ninety of them should be right. A model whose confidence matches how often it is right is called calibrated.

`distill` calibrates the student for you. It looks at the student's confidence on rows it was not trained on, then fits one number, called a temperature, that scales the confidence up or down to match. The detail is under [How it was measured](#how-it-was-measured).

An honest confidence lets you set a bar. At or above the bar, you use the student's answer. Below it, you ask the teacher. hunch calls that bar [`act`](/concepts#act-sets-a-bar-for-review), and you set it for each question in the spec.

Whether the student's confidence is honest is still a claim at this point. The test, after training, checks it on fresh rows.

## Train a student

The example is the [BANKING77 spec](/cookbooks/banking-intents), in `prototype/examples/banking77` of the [hunch repository](https://github.com/oneryalcin/hunch). An earlier `hunch run` had sent 3,000 unlabelled messages to Jev, so its answers were in the store.

On your own spec, do the same first. `hunch run` it on the rows you have, so that the store holds answers to learn from.

Install the extra once. It brings fastembed and its runtime: onnxruntime, tokenizers and about 20 other packages.

```bash
uv add "hunch-ai[distill]"
```

Then distill:

```bash
cd prototype/examples/banking77
hunch distill intent.yml --source banking77_unlabeled.csv
```

```text
  encoding 3000 rows with mdbr-leaf-mt
intent: distilled to distilled/intent@1a70f2d8
  intent: 3000 answers, 77 classes; rarest: 'contactless_not_working' (7)  ← few examples: measure what it misses (hunch test --model) before trusting it
measure it (free, local): hunch test intent.yml --model distilled:distilled/intent@1a70f2d8
use it: model: distilled:distilled/intent@1a70f2d8, with escalate: {model: jev-1.13.0} and act on each question, so answers it isn't sure of go to jev-1.13.0
```

Here is what each line says.

- **`encoding 3000 rows with mdbr-leaf-mt`** The encoder reads each message once. On its first run it downloads, 92 MB, to `~/.cache/hunch/encoders` or to the folder `FASTEMBED_CACHE_PATH` names.
- **`distilled to distilled/intent@1a70f2d8`** This is the student's folder. Its name ends in a hash of its contents, so a different student gets a different folder.
- **`3000 answers, 77 classes`** This is what the layer learned from. A class is one possible answer.
- **`rarest: 'contactless_not_working' (7)`** Only seven of the 3,000 lessons were about this intent. hunch warns below 30, because a student rarely learns an answer from so few examples.
- **The last two lines** are the next two steps: measure the student, then use it.

## Test it on rows it never saw

A student can memorise its lessons. Asked about a message it trained on, it looks better than it is. An honest test uses rows the student has never seen, the way an exam uses new questions.

That is what held-back rows are for. In this example the 3,000 lessons had no gold, so there was nothing to hold back. The example keeps 385 other messages aside for the test instead, each with an answer key.

A distilled model is an engine like any other, named `distilled:<folder>`, so `hunch test` measures it. `--max-cost 0` forbids any paid request, so the test cannot cost anything.

```bash
hunch test intent.yml --source banking77_holdout.csv --model distilled:distilled/intent@1a70f2d8 --max-cost 0
```

```text
intent (choice, 385 rows)
  PASS accuracy 92.7% (raw source gold: 86.5%) (min 85%)
  PASS calibration error 0.017 (raw source gold: 0.053) (max 0.05)
       dial   automated   wrong among automated
       0.70       89.9%                    3.2%
       0.80       85.2%                    2.4%
       0.90       77.1%                    1.3%  ← act
       0.95       68.1%                    0.8%
```

Read it from the top.

**Accuracy.** Answering every message by itself, the student is right on 92.7%. Jev is right on 97.9% of the same messages. The student is the weaker model, as you would expect.

The 86.5% in brackets is the score against the dataset's original answer key. Reviewers found that key wrong on some messages, and hunch grades against their corrections. The [cookbook](/cookbooks/banking-intents) tells that story.

**Calibration error.** This is the average gap between how sure the student said it was and how often it was right. Here it is 0.017, under the spec's limit of 0.05, so the student's confidence held up on these rows.

**The dial.** This table shows what each setting of `act` would do. With `act` at 0.90, the student is confident enough on 77.1% of the messages, and wrong on 1.3% of those, which is 4 of 297. The arrow marks the `act` the spec has now.

Look at where the mistakes are. The student is wrong on 28 of the 385 messages, and it is unsure about most of those 28. With `act` at 0.90, 24 of them fall below the bar, where the teacher will answer instead. That is why a weaker model can do most of the work.

Drag the bar and watch the trade. Each dot is one of the 385 messages.

<ActDial rows={STUDENT_ROWS} initial={0.9} student />

A higher bar leaves the student fewer messages and fewer mistakes. A lower bar does the opposite. The dial only describes these 385 messages, so choose the bar on rows like the ones your app will see.

You don't have to keep track of which rows a student has seen. `distill` records every row it trained on, and `hunch test` grades a student only on the others.

## Put the teacher behind it

The student should answer what it is sure of, and the teacher should answer the rest. Two lines in the spec do that. `intent_distilled.yml` is `intent.yml` with the student in front and Jev behind it:

```yaml intent_distilled.yml
model: distilled:distilled/intent@1a70f2d8
questions:
  intent:
    # type, instructions, criteria as in intent.yml
    act: 0.90
    escalate: {model: jev-1.13.0}    # below 0.90, the teacher answers
```

[`act`](/concepts#act-sets-a-bar-for-review) is the bar. `escalate` names the model that answers below it.

![A local student answers first. At or above act its answer is used; below it, Jev answers instead. Both paths are measured on held-out rows.](/images/distill-fallback.svg)

Test the pair as you tested the student:

```bash
hunch test intent_distilled.yml --source banking77_holdout.csv --max-cost 0
```

```text
intent (choice, 385 rows)
  PASS estimated accuracy 95.5% (95% CI 88.3%–97.6%) from reviews of 60/342 agreeing rows, 43/43 disagreeing rows (min 85%)
  PASS calibration error 0.030 (raw source gold: 0.058) (max 0.05)
  PASS accuracy among auto-acted 98.8% on 88% of rows at act=0.9 (min 97%)
  PASS order stability: 0/300 answers flip (0.0%, 0 within noise band), mean |Δp| of original answer 0.000 (max flip rate 5%)
  escalated to jev-1.13.0: 88/385 rows use its answer, 42 of them confident enough to act; right on 83/88 with gold
```

Start with the last line. 88 of the 385 messages went to Jev. The other 297, more than three in four, were answered on this machine.

The third line counts the answers confident enough to act on: the student's 297, and 42 of Jev's 88. Together that is 88% of the messages, and 98.8% of those answers were right. (The two 88s are a coincidence.)

Was anything lost by putting the student in front? Graded against the reviewed answer key, the pair was right on 97.7% of the messages and Jev alone on 97.9%. That is one message apart.

That grading trusts the answer key wherever no reviewer has looked. The first line of the output does not. It is an [estimate](/reference/how-the-numbers-work#estimated-accuracy) built from the reviewed rows alone, and it puts the pair at 95.5%. Jev alone, measured the same way, is at 95.8%. Both come with a range of about 88% to 98%, because 385 messages is a small sample.

The other lines are the spec's remaining [tests](/reference/spec#tests).

Escalation works a little differently behind a student. Between two ordinary engines, the second answer replaces the first only if it clears `act` itself. Behind a student, the teacher's answer always replaces it, because the teacher is the better model.

## A bigger store teaches a better student, up to a point

How many answers does a student need? We trained three students on growing piles of Jev's answers and tested each on the same 385 messages.

| Jev's answers learned from | answered locally at 0.9 | right, with Jev behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 770 | 64.9% | 97.4% |
| 1,770 | 79.5% | 97.7% |
| 3,770 | 78.7% | 97.7% |

A thousand more answers moved the local share from 65% to 80%. The two thousand after that changed nothing you could measure on 385 messages.

Every run and every review adds to your store. Both `hunch distill` and `hunch test --model distilled:…` are free to repeat, so you can train a newer student and check whether it keeps more.

## When a student is the wrong tool

**A rare answer that is costly to miss.** The [command guard](/guides/guard-your-agent) decides whether a shell command needs a person before it runs. About 2% of commands do, so its student had learned from only 11 destructive commands. We tested that student, with Jev behind it, on commands it had never seen and that a [panel of reviewers](/cookbooks/command-guard) had labelled. It let through 2 of 6 destructive commands and 1 of 12 that send data out. Jev alone let through none.

That student read with the MiniLM encoder. hunch has four [encoders](#choose-the-encoder), and a different one narrows the gap without closing it. In a larger check over all 1,268 of the guard's commands, its three questions had 121 yes answers between them. Depending on the encoder, the student said a confident no to between 10 and 17 of those. At that confidence Jev is never asked, so nothing catches the miss.

`distill` flags rare answers when it trains, and `hunch test --model distilled:…` shows how many confident answers were wrong. For a guard like that, keep the engine until the store holds many more of the rare answer.

**An answer that hangs on an exact detail.** In the [product matching cookbook](/cookbooks/product-matching), a student barely beat a coin flip at telling matching listings from different ones. It reads both listings as one piece of text, and loses whether `EX85` and `EX81` are the same code.

**A question you have changed.** A student answers only the questions it was trained on, word for word. Change a question and the student refuses to answer until you distill again. [`hunch diff`](/guides/change-a-spec) against the old engine then shows which answers the new student changed.

## Choose the encoder

The student on this page reads with `mdbr-leaf-mt`, the default. hunch can use three other encoders, and `--encoder` picks one:

```bash
hunch distill intent.yml --source banking77_unlabeled.csv --encoder gte-modernbert-base
```

Three questions settle the choice for most specs.

**Is your text in English?** Three of the four encoders are built for English. For other languages, use `embeddinggemma-2`. Nothing on this page was measured on text in another language.

**How long is a row?** An encoder reads up to a fixed number of tokens, and a token is a word or a piece of one. It ignores anything past its limit. The default reads 512 tokens, `embeddinggemma-2` reads 1,024 and `gte-modernbert-base` reads 2,048.

**How long can an answer take?** The default answers a short message in 3 ms and uses 0.2 GB of memory. The two larger encoders take 16 to 58 ms and about 1 GB.

If none of these moves you, stay on the default. To compare two encoders on your own rows, distill with each and run `hunch test --model` on both. Both steps are free.

Here is everything we measured. The four students learned from the same 3,000 answers. Times are on one Apple M1 Pro's CPU, one row at a time, and every measurement is on English text.

BANKING77 is a public benchmark, and an encoder that met it in training would look better here than on your data. Two checks found no sign of that for mdbr-leaf-mt, MiniLM and gte-modernbert-base. They could not rule it out for EmbeddingGemma 2.

The model folder records its encoder. A student keeps reading with the encoder it was trained on, so one distilled on MiniLM before the default changed still runs on MiniLM.

## What an answer costs

Inside a running app, a new answer takes 3 ms for a short message and 17 ms for a long shell command, with the default encoder. The larger encoders take longer, as [the table](#choose-the-encoder) shows.

A fresh process first pays about 0.3 s to load the encoder. So a hook that starts a new process for every command gains no speed from a student. It saves the cost and the network call.

A distilled answer costs nothing. It is stored like any other answer, so a row seen before comes from the store.

## Run it in a web page

A student is small enough to run in a visitor's browser. The answers it is sure of then never leave their machine, and only the rest reach your server.

[Run a student in a web page](/guides/student-in-a-web-page) covers the export, the JavaScript module, and what to check before you ship it. A visitor can change what their own browser answers, so read that page before you use a browser student for anything that protects something.

## How it was measured

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="The data and the machine">
    BANKING77 is a public set of bank customers' messages (PolyAI, CC BY 4.0). The student learned from Jev's answers to 3,000 unlabelled messages and was tested on 385 others.

    Not seen means not seen as that exact text. 3 of the 385 test messages differ from a training message only in capital letters or punctuation.

    Every time on this page is from one Apple M1 Pro's CPU, one row at a time. Every measurement is on English text.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How much 385 messages can tell you">
    The student was wrong on 4 of the 297 messages it kept, which is 1.3%. On a sample that size the true rate could be anywhere from about 0.5% to 3.4% (a 95% Wilson interval).

    0.90 is the `act` the example's spec already had for Jev.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What the layer is, exactly">
    The layer is a softmax regression, also called multinomial logistic regression. It holds one weight for each of the encoder's numbers and each answer, and one more number per answer.

    It learns the answer the teacher chose, not the teacher's probabilities. We tried teaching it the probabilities instead, and the student was no better. On the 385 bank messages it was right on 92.2%, against 92.7%. On the command guard it missed 14 of the 121 yes answers, against 15, when both students kept the same share of the work.

    Each possible answer counts equally in training, however rare it is, so that a student does not learn to ignore a rare one.

    Closeness on this page is the cosine similarity of two messages' lists of numbers.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How confidence is calibrated">
    `distill` splits the lessons into five parts. Five times, it trains a layer on four parts and records what that layer says about the fifth. The result is a prediction for every lesson from a layer that never saw it.

    It then fits a temperature: the one number that makes those predictions' confidence match how often they were right. The student's answers stay the same, and only their confidence moves.

    If those predictions hold fewer than 10 mistakes, there is too little to fit, and the confidence is left as it is.

    The answers those predictions are compared with are the lessons' own: gold where a row has it, and the teacher's elsewhere. So on lessons without gold, calibrated means calibrated to agreeing with the teacher. `hunch test`, on rows that have gold, is what checks the confidence against the truth.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which rows are held back">
    One in five rows that have gold is held back, chosen by a hash of the row's text, so the same rows are held back every time. Rows without gold are all used for training, because a test cannot grade them anyway.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The growing-store table">
    The three students were trained by a script that calls hunch's own training. The first learned from the 770 development messages, the second from those and 1,000 of the unlabelled ones, and the third from those and all 3,000.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Matched quality">
    Each of the 3,000 training messages is answered by a student trained without it. Each student's bar is then set at the lowest confidence where it agrees with Jev on at least 97% of what it answers, with a safety margin: the low end of a 95% Wilson interval has to clear 97%. The row shows the share each student answers at its bar.

    At the bars this picked, the students agreed with Jev on 97.8% to 97.9% of what they answered. The rule also asks for 80% agreement on answers other than the commonest one, which never decided a bar here.

    This is the larger sample. It also compares more fairly than one fixed bar, because the four encoders' confidences are calibrated differently. A script that calls hunch's own training measured it.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="The command guard rows">
    The guard is data whose labels no encoder has seen. It asks three yes/no questions of 1,268 commands, with 121 yes answers in all. Every command is answered by a student trained without it.

    A confident miss is a no at 0.9 or above where the review panel said yes, or where Jev said yes and the panel gave no verdict. A gap of a few misses between encoders is within chance.

    The 2 of 6 and 1 of 12 in [When a student is the wrong tool](#when-a-student-is-the-wrong-tool) are from a MiniLM student with Jev behind it at `act: 0.9`, on commands the panel had labelled. Six and twelve commands are very few. That result shows that misses happen, not how often.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Long rows cost time">
    gte-modernbert-base took 0.9 s for a 1,024-token row and 2.3 s for 2,048. The model itself accepts 8,192 tokens, which took 31 s and 8 GB here, so hunch stops it at 2,048. EmbeddingGemma 2 accepts as many, and hunch stops it at 1,024.

    Use [`clip`](/reference/spec) to send less of a long column.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why two encoders are not compressed">
    Each encoder is pinned to one revision and runs on the onnxruntime the `distill` extra already installs.

    hunch uses a compressed, 8-bit build of an encoder only if it computes the same numbers everywhere: on an Apple chip, on the Intel code path and in a browser. EmbeddingGemma 2's 8-bit build did, on every row.

    The 8-bit builds of mdbr-leaf-mt (23 MB) and gte-modernbert-base (150 MB) did not. Between the Apple chip and the Intel code path they differed on 43 and 205 of 385 rows, so hunch uses their full-precision builds.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
